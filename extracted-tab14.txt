[HEADING_2] The Science of Drying, Simply Explained
[NORMAL_TEXT] Drying is about moving water from wet materials into the air, then out of the air and out of the house. Four things control how fast that happens:
[HEADING_3] Airflow
[NORMAL_TEXT] Air movers blow across wet surfaces to speed evaporation. They are angled along walls and under cabinets to reach the places water hides.
[HEADING_3] Humidity
[NORMAL_TEXT] Commercial low-grain refrigerant (LGR) and desiccant dehumidifiers pull the evaporated water out of the air. Without them, humid Apopka air just puts moisture back into the materials.
[HEADING_3] Temperature
[NORMAL_TEXT] Warm air holds more moisture, so a controlled temperature speeds drying. Too cold and drying stalls; too hot without dehumidification and moisture moves into dry areas.
[HEADING_3] Time
[NORMAL_TEXT] Even with the right equipment, dense materials like concrete and hardwood release moisture slowly. Pulling equipment too early is the most common reason drying jobs fail.

[HEADING_2] Moisture Readings: How We Know It Is Dry
[NORMAL_TEXT] Every drying job starts by taking readings of unaffected materials in the home, such as a dry wall in another room. That sets the dry goal for each material. Then wet materials are read every day and logged.
[NORMAL_TEXT] Drywall is dry when it matches the readings of unaffected drywall in the same home.
[NORMAL_TEXT] Wood framing and subfloor are dry when they reach a stable reading close to the dry goal.
[NORMAL_TEXT] Concrete slabs are tested before new flooring is installed, since flooring manufacturers have their own moisture limits.
[NORMAL_TEXT] Relative humidity and temperature are logged in the drying area and outside the home.
[NORMAL_TEXT] Those daily moisture readings become part of your file, showing your insurer and future buyers that the home was dried properly.

[NORMAL_TEXT] Drywall can often be dried in place when the water was clean, extraction started quickly and the drywall is still firm. Drywall usually needs to be replaced when:

[HEADING_2] Why Equipment Placement Matters
[NORMAL_TEXT] Drying is not about putting as many fans in a room as possible. Too many air movers without enough dehumidification just raise humidity and can push moisture into dry areas. Too few leave wet spots that turn into mold. A drying plan matches the number of air movers to the wet surfaces and the dehumidifier capacity to the room size and water load. Air movers are angled to create a circular airflow along wet walls. Dehumidifiers are placed so they can discharge water to a sink, tub or outside. The plan is adjusted each day based on readings, removing equipment from areas that are dry and focusing on areas that are not.
