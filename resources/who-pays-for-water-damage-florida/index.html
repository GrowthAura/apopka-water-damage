Who Pays for Water Damage in Florida? HOA, Landlord & Neighbors

(407) 787-9139Request Emergency Help

When water damage crosses from one property to another, or happens in a rental, condo or HOA community, the first question is often who pays for it. The answer depends on where the water came from, what caused it, the type of property and the insurance policies and governing documents involved. This guide explains the general rules in Florida so you know where to start. It is general information, not legal advice.

Who pays when water comes from the unit above?

Usually the policy covering the unit where the leak started responds first, but it can depend on negligence, condo documents and which parts of the building were damaged.

If a neighbor's washing machine hose bursts and water comes through your ceiling, their HO-6 or homeowners policy may cover the damage to your unit if they were negligent. If the cause was sudden and no one was at fault, each owner's own policy often covers their own unit, and the association's master policy may cover parts of the building it is responsible for. In practice, it is common to file with your own insurer and let the insurers sort out who reimburses whom.

The Basic Rule: It Depends on the Source and the Cause

Across most Florida water damage situations, three questions guide who pays:

  • Where did the water come from? A pipe inside your unit, a neighbor's unit, a common element, or outside?
  • What caused it? A sudden failure, long-term neglect, a storm or a flood?
  • Who is responsible for that part of the building? You, a neighbor, the association or the landlord?
  • Insurance policies and governing documents then decide who files and who pays.

Condo Water Damage From the Unit Above

Florida condominiums are governed by Chapter 718 of the Florida Statutes and the association's declaration. In general:

The association's master policy covers the building structure and common elements as defined by law and the declaration.

Unit owners are generally responsible for their own interior finishes and improvements, such as flooring, cabinets, countertops, appliances, water heaters and personal property, and insure them with an HO-6 policy.

  • If the leak came from a common element, like a main stack or the roof, the association is often responsible for the repair to that element.
  • If an owner was negligent, such as leaving a tub running or ignoring a known leak, their policy may be responsible for damage to other units.
  • Condo documents vary a lot, so always check your declaration and ask your association manager.

HOA Communities and Townhomes

In most single-family HOA communities in Apopka, governed by Chapter 720, the homeowner owns and insures their entire house, and the HOA is responsible only for common areas like clubhouses, pools and landscaping. Water damage inside your home is usually your responsibility and your homeowners policy.

Townhomes can be different. Some townhome associations insure roofs or exterior walls under a master policy, while others leave everything to the owner. Shared walls between townhomes mean a leak can affect two homes. Check your HOA documents to see who insures what.

Landlord vs Tenant Water Damage in Florida

Florida landlord-tenant law generally requires landlords to maintain the plumbing and keep the building in good repair. In general:

  • The landlord is usually responsible for repairing the building and its systems, such as pipes, water heaters and the roof, and for damage to the structure.
  • The landlord's policy typically covers the building, not the tenant's belongings.
  • The tenant's renters insurance usually covers the tenant's personal property and may cover temporary housing if the unit is unlivable.
  • If the tenant caused the damage through negligence, such as overflowing a tub, the tenant may be responsible.
  • Tenants should report leaks to the landlord in writing right away. Landlords should respond quickly, because delays can lead to mold and bigger losses.

Rental Water Damage: Short-Term Rentals

Short-term rentals near Wekiwa Springs, Kelly Park and the Orlando area add guests to the picture. Guests may not report small leaks, and owners may not visit for days. Responsibility usually falls on the owner, who should carry a policy that covers short-term rental use. Leak sensors, labeled shut-off valves and regular inspections between guests prevent most large losses.

Water From a Neighbor's Yard or Property

Water that runs off a neighbor's property during heavy rain is generally treated as surface water or flooding, which standard homeowners policies usually exclude. If a neighbor's broken irrigation line or pool leak floods your yard or home, their liability coverage may apply if they were negligent. These situations can be complex; documentation of the source is key.

What to Do First, No Matter Who Pays

  • Stop the water if you can, or notify whoever controls the source.
  • Photograph and video the damage and the source.
  • Report it to your own insurer and, if relevant, your landlord or association in writing.
  • Get the water extracted and the area dried right away; waiting causes mold and can hurt any claim.
  • Keep receipts and a written record of who you spoke with and when.

How Documentation Helps Settle Who Pays

When more than one party is involved, the party with the best documentation usually has the easiest time. Moisture maps show where water traveled, photos show the source, and daily drying logs show the damage was handled properly. Frequently Asked Questions

My upstairs neighbor's toilet leaked into my condo. Whose insurance pays?

+

Often you file with your own HO-6 policy, and your insurer may seek reimbursement from the neighbor's insurer if they were negligent. Check your condo documents.

Does my HOA pay for water damage inside my house?

+

In most single-family HOA communities, no. The homeowner insures the house. HOAs usually cover only common areas.

Who pays for water damage in a rental in Florida?

+

The landlord usually handles repairs to the building, and the tenant's renters policy covers the tenant's belongings, unless the tenant caused the damage.
